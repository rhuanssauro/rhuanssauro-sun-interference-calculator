What Sun Interference is

Intelsat’s Sun Interference Background states the cause plainly: the Sun passing directly behind a geostationary satellite as seen from a receiving earth station. For several minutes each day during the equinox season (February/March and September/October), the Sun crosses the equatorial plane used by GSO spacecraft. Apparent solar path then puts the solar disk in the earth station’s line of sight. The Sun is an intense thermal-noise source at the same radio frequencies the satellite uses, so antenna noise temperature rises, G/T falls, and C/N (or C/I) degrades — a sun fade, sun transit, or sun outage.

When it happens

  • Northern hemisphere: several days just prior to the March equinox and just after the September equinox.
  • Southern hemisphere (Macaé): the reverse — after March, before September.
  • Sites west of the satellite see morning windows; sites east see afternoon windows.
  • Larger dishes have narrower beams, so the window is shorter. Smaller VSAT antennas stay in outage longer.

Today’s date in this workspace is 10 September 2026, at the end of the Southern-hemisphere pre-equinox window. A Macaé verdict of “out of season” or a last remaining day is a valid owner-style result, not an empty dashboard.

Method used here (owner geometry, not a portal scrape)

Intelsat, Hispasat, and Telesat publish web calculators. They do not publish a public JSON API. This page does not log into MyIntelsat or scrape those UIs. It applies the method those owners document, on public orbital positions.

  • Hispasat: “This calculator generates estimates based exclusively on geometric angles, and does not consider parameters such as link RF margin, antenna un-pointing, performance.”
  • Intelsat: the calculator “computes the antenna beam width and determines the interference duration” from earth-station size, band, and location.
  • ITU-R S.1525-1: 3 dB beamwidth θ3dB = 70 λ / D. Optical solar diameter ≈ 0.48°. Hour angle of the Sun ≈ 0.25° per minute. Maximum duration ≈ (θ3dB + 0.48) / 0.25 minutes.
  • This checker: outage while the solar disk (~0.5°) is inside the receive 3 dB beam — half beamwidth + 0.25° solar radius. Verdict is impacted / not impacted / not in view / out of season, plus UTC start, end, and duration when a window exists.

South America vendor slots

Hispasat’s own calculator lists 30°W (30W-5, 30W-6), 36°W (36W-1), 55.5°W (55W-2), 61°W (Amazonas 2, 3, 5, Nexus), 70°W (70W-1), and 74°W (74W-1). Telesat’s Telstar 19V (~63°W), 11N (~37.5°W) and 12V (~15°W) are Atlantic/Americas birds. Intelsat’s Atlantic fleet (IS-14 45°W, IS-21 58°W, IS-34 55.5°W, IS-35e 34.5°W, IS-37e 18°W, and others) is tagged in the full in-view set, not shown as if they were the only satellites that exist.

Orbital longitudes on the belt come from Celestrak GP GROUP=geo OMM JSON (Earth-fixed longitude = RAAN + argument of perigee + mean anomaly − GMST at epoch). A dated snapshot is bundled so the page works on file://. Live refresh is attempted only when the origin is not a local file (Celestrak CORS and their one-download-per-update policy).
