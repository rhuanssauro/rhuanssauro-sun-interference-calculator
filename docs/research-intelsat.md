## 2. Sun Interference Background PDF — required quotes

**Source:** <https://my.intelsat.com/resource/si/Sun_Interference_Background.pdf>  
**Status:** HTTP 200, 44 900 bytes, one page. Title: *Sun Interference Background*. Figure 1 caption still says “(to be updated by Graphics)”.

### Cause

> Satellite-based communication is affected by sun interference which is caused by the sun passing directly behind a geostationary satellite as seen from a receiving earth station, see Figure 1. Depending on the receive antenna size, its efficiency and the frequency band used, this interference can cause degradation in quality of service or a complete service outage.

> For several minutes each day for several days during the equinox season (February/March and September/October), the sun passes through the equatorial plane which is used by geostationary satellites. At these times, the apparent path of the sun across the sky takes it directly behind the satellite making it appear in the beam width of a receive earth station’s line-of-sight.

> The heat emitted by the sun is an intense source of thermal noise radiated at the same frequency range communication satellites use: radio frequencies (RF), and the receive antenna noise temperature rises significantly. A sun outage occurs because the earth station cannot distinguish between the energy from the sun and its intended communication signal. This can be expressed as a reduced Carrier-to-Interference (C/I) ratio, a degraded Carrier-to-Noise (C/N) ratio or as an increased antenna noise temperature (and thus reduced receive antenna G/T) as the Interference (Noise) increases during the sun transit.

### Northern vs southern hemisphere timing

> For earth stations located in the northern hemisphere, this interference occurs for several days just prior to the spring equinox and just after the autumnal equinox (exactly reversed for the southern hemisphere).

So for a Brazilian (southern-hemisphere) earth station the PDF’s rule is the reverse of the NH pattern: several days **just after** the March equinox and **just prior** to the September equinox. That is consistent with the calculator’s note that season names are Northern-Hemisphere timing.

### Morning vs afternoon vs site longitude relative to the satellite

> In general, sun interference occurs during the morning hours for earth stations situated at longitudes west of the sub-satellite point, while they occur during the afternoon for those earth stations that are located east of the satellite’s orbital location.

### Calculator uses antenna beamwidth

> Since solar interference occurs when the sun appears within the receive earth station’s line-of-sight, the Sun Interference Calculator computes the antenna beam width and determines the interference duration. This is achieved by determining the angle of visibility based on the receive earth station size, frequency band used and antenna location; from there, the software calculates the time at which the sun enters into the line-of-sight of the earth station, thus determining when interference starts based on proper antenna pointing, and accurately predicts the resulting daily interference duration. The calculator allows the user to determine outage times in advance allowing you to set expectations for your customers.

Ephemeris/Keplerian cadence (identical wording on both MyIntelsat pages):

> We update the data for each satellite on a weekly basis, usually on Thursday and Friday. The start dates are Saturday and Sunday, respectively, and are valid for a period of seven days from epoch. All times are in UTC.

> We provide additional data when station-keeping or relocation maneuvers are scheduled - typically one to three messages per month. Maneuver files may be issued any day of the week, and become effective at the time of the maneuver. They remain effective until superseded by another maneuver update file or by the next set of routine weekly data.

**File URL pattern (Intelsat ephemeris):** `/Resource/Ephemeris/{stem}.txt`  
Stems look like `i_aor_{c\|e\|m\|w\|x}_{lonE}_{name}_{yyyymmdd}_{hhmmss}` where `c` = Center of Box, `e` = ECF, `m` = maneuvers, `w` = weekly, `x` = weekly previous; `i_` = Intelsat-owned, `n_` = leased capacity.

**File URL pattern (Intelsat Keplerian):** `/Resource/Keplerian/kep_{m\|w\|x}_{scid}_{yyyymmdd}_{hhmmss}.txt`

Keplerian files are **not** NORAD TLEs. A weekly IS-21 file begins:

```
ZCZC 4521COE
sc4521COE
.11param
 SATELLITE NUMBER = 4521
 DESCRIPTION:           WEEKLY 11-PARAMETER DATA
```

## 5. Implications for a Brazil sun-outage calculator

1. **Season:** for southern-hemisphere sites the PDF reverses the NH equinox rule. The MyIntelsat UI still names seasons in NH terms.
2. **Time of day:** Brazil lies **west** of AOR sub-satellite points from ~18°W to ~1°W and **east or mixed** relative to 58°W–45°W. The PDF’s morning/afternoon rule is longitude of the **earth station versus the satellite**, not “Brazil = morning”.
3. **Orbit source for “current season”:** Intelsat’s own tool prefers **ephemeris / 11-parameter Keplerian** over nominal slot; Celestrak `FILE=intelsat` is the public SGP4 fit of that ephemeris (with `[PM]` maneuver sets). Nominal longitude is what remains if the season is not the current outage season.
4. **Beamwidth:** duration is an antenna-size / frequency / location visibility angle, not an RF C/N calculation. The UI adds a ±10 minute operational margin on top of that geometry.
